# Day 24 — When Your Heart Is Questioned

> *“Then Mary wept and said to Peter, ‘My brother Peter, what do you think?’”*
>
> *“‘Do you think that I thought this up myself in my heart, or that I am lying about the Savior?’”*

Mary weeps. The detail is easy to move past because the argument around her is so compelling, but the text allows us to see what the argument costs. Peter has not merely questioned an interpretation; from Mary's perspective, he has questioned the integrity of something intimate and sacred. Her tears remind us that theological disagreements happen inside human bodies and relationships.

Mary does not respond by attacking Peter. She calls him “my brother Peter.” Somehow the relationship remains present even in the wound. Then she asks him directly what he believes about her: Do you think I invented this? Do you think I am lying? Her response brings the conversation beneath the surface disagreement and exposes the more painful question underneath it—*Do you trust me?*

Many conflicts have this hidden layer. We begin by disagreeing about an event, an idea, or a memory, but before long something more personal is at stake. We hear not simply, “I see this differently,” but “Your experience cannot be trusted.” We feel misunderstood, dismissed, or suspected. At that point, winning the argument rarely heals what has actually been wounded.

There is courage in Mary's tears. She does not hide her pain in order to appear spiritually advanced. The woman who just described a soul passing beyond Desire, Ignorance, and Wrath is still capable of being hurt by someone she considers a brother. Freedom does not make us invulnerable. Perhaps it makes us more able to acknowledge vulnerability without allowing it to harden into retaliation.

Mary's response offers another way through conflict. She names what hurts without striking back. She asks a question rather than delivering a verdict. She leaves Peter room to recognize what his suspicion implies.

We may not always receive the answer we hope for when we speak this openly. But there is something freeing about refusing both silence and revenge. We can say, gently and truthfully, *This wounded me. Is this really what you believe about me?* Sometimes reconciliation begins not with better arguments, but with allowing another person to see the heart their words have touched.

**Prayer**

> God who meets me in vulnerability, give me courage to speak honestly when I have been wounded. Keep pain from hardening into contempt, and keep peacekeeping from becoming silence about what matters. Help me remember the humanity of the person before me even when we disagree. Teach me to tell the truth with tenderness and to leave room for understanding to grow. Amen.
