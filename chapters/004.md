## Day 4 — The Passion of Attachment

> *“Let the one who can understand, understand.”*
>
> *“Matter gave birth to a passion that has no equal, which proceeded from something contrary to nature. Then there arises a disturbance in the whole body.”*

There are desires that pass through us lightly. We notice them, perhaps enjoy them, and then watch them disappear. But there are other desires that seem to take hold of us. They promise that something just beyond our reach will finally settle us, and the more urgently we pursue it, the more restless we become. The Gospel of Mary calls this a passion, something powerful enough to create a disturbance in the whole person.

We often assume that peace will come when the desire is satisfied. If we can acquire the thing, receive the recognition, taste the pleasure, resolve the uncertainty, or finally arrange life according to our wishes, then perhaps we can rest. Sometimes satisfaction does bring relief, but we soon discover how temporary that relief can be. Another desire appears, another uncertainty arises, and the mind begins reaching again.

The problem is not desire itself. Hunger draws us toward food, loneliness toward companionship, curiosity toward discovery, and love toward another person. Desire belongs to being alive. But desire becomes something different when we can no longer allow it to remain unanswered, when wanting quietly turns into needing and the possibility of not receiving what we want begins to disturb our inner world.

Perhaps this is why the Savior begins by saying, “Let the one who can understand, understand.” This kind of understanding cannot remain theoretical. We have to recognize the movement in ourselves: the tightening of the body, the rehearsing of thoughts, the restless reaching toward whatever promises relief. If we can notice that movement without immediately obeying it, we may discover a small space between desire and action.

That space can become a place of freedom. We can breathe while wanting. We can feel longing without treating longing as an emergency. We may even discover that beneath the object of our desire lies a deeper longing—for comfort, belonging, safety, love, or simply the assurance that this moment is enough. When we stop demanding that every desire be satisfied, the disturbance begins to quiet, and we become capable of hearing what the longing was trying to tell us.

**Prayer**

> God of peace, meet me in the restless places within me. When desire becomes urgency, help me remain present long enough to hear what lies beneath it. Teach me that I can want without grasping, long without being ruled by longing, and rest even when something remains unfinished. In the space between desire and action, help me discover freedom. Amen.
