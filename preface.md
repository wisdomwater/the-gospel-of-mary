## Preface

The Gospel of Mary comes to us wounded.

Its first six pages are gone. We do not know how the gospel began, how its author introduced Mary, or what conversation led to the words that first survive. We simply arrive in the middle of things, listening as the Savior answers a question already underway. Then, just as Mary begins recounting a private revelation she received from him, four more pages disappear. When the manuscript resumes, her vision is already in motion.

There is something strangely appropriate about meeting this gospel in fragments. We are accustomed to thinking of sacred texts as things handed down intact, their words safely carried from one generation to another. The Gospel of Mary asks something different of us. We must listen through absence, resisting the temptation to fill the silence with what we wish had been there.

## A Gospel Recovered

The principal surviving copy of the Gospel of Mary is preserved in a manuscript known today as the Berlin Codex, or *Papyrus Berolinensis 8502*. Written in Coptic and generally dated to the fifth century, the codex contains four early Christian works. The Gospel of Mary appears first, followed by the *Apocryphon of John*, the *Sophia of Jesus Christ*, and a version of the *Acts of Peter*.

The manuscript emerged into modern view in the late nineteenth century. It was acquired in Cairo in 1896 and eventually made its way to Berlin, but political and scholarly circumstances delayed its publication for decades. By the time the Gospel of Mary became widely available to scholars in the twentieth century, the landscape of early Christianity was already being transformed by other manuscript discoveries, most famously the collection found near Nag Hammadi in Egypt.

Yet the Berlin Codex is not our only witness to Mary's Gospel. Two much smaller Greek fragments have also survived, both generally dated several centuries earlier than the Coptic manuscript. They overlap portions of the text we already possess rather than restoring the missing pages, but their existence matters. They show that the Gospel of Mary was not simply a late Coptic composition. Some form of the text was circulating in Greek by at least the third century.

How widely it circulated, who read it, and precisely how particular Christian communities used it are much harder questions to answer. We should be careful not to imagine a clearly defined “community of Mary” for which we have no direct evidence. What we can say is simpler and perhaps more compelling: people copied this gospel. They preserved it in different languages and manuscripts across centuries. Someone considered these words worth the considerable labor required to keep them alive.

## The Pages That Are Gone

The surviving Coptic manuscript begins on page seven. Pages one through six have been lost, which means that we possess neither the original opening nor the setting it may have provided. We enter while the Savior is already teaching his disciples about matter, sin, suffering, and the return of all things to their root.

Pages seven through ten survive. Then four more pages—eleven through fourteen—are missing.

The timing of this second loss is almost painful. Mary has just begun describing a vision of the Savior. She asks whether a vision is perceived through the soul or the spirit, and the Savior answers that it is the *mind*—the inner faculty of perception—that sees. Then the manuscript disappears. Four pages later, we return to find the soul ascending through powers that attempt to restrain it.

We do not know what happened in between.

There have naturally been attempts to understand what the missing material might have contained, but in these readings we will allow the silence to remain. The gap itself will become part of our encounter with Mary. There is humility in receiving an incomplete text as incomplete, allowing what has been lost to remain beyond our grasp.

Perhaps this is not so different from the way we receive our own lives. Few of us possess a complete explanation for the path that brought us here. Some chapters make sense only in retrospect, while others remain unresolved. There are questions we carry for years, relationships whose endings never become entirely clear, and experiences whose meaning cannot be reconstructed no matter how many times we revisit them.

The missing pages remind us that something need not be complete to be meaningful.

## Mary and the Question of Authority

The Mary of this gospel emerges most clearly after the Savior departs. The disciples are frightened. They know what happened to their teacher and wonder what will happen if they carry his message into the world. They weep and hesitate.

Mary stands.

She reminds them that grace will remain with them. She turns their hearts toward the Good. And when Peter asks her to share what she knows of the Savior's teaching, she begins describing a revelation that the others have not heard.

What follows is one of the most fascinating disputes in early Christian literature. Andrew questions the teaching because it seems strange to him. Peter goes further, asking whether the Savior would really have spoken privately with a woman and whether the others are now expected to listen to her. Mary weeps and asks whether Peter believes she has invented the teaching or lied about the Savior.

Finally Levi intervenes. He rebukes Peter for becoming adversarial toward Mary and reminds him that if the Savior considered her worthy, Peter has little standing to reject her.

It is tempting to turn this scene into a simple contest between Mary and Peter, or between two clearly defined forms of Christianity. The historical reality was almost certainly more complicated than that. Early Christianity contained overlapping communities, traditions, arguments, practices, and interpretations of Jesus, and the surviving evidence rarely allows us to draw neat boundaries between them.

But the tension preserved in Mary's Gospel is real. Who gets to speak? Whose experience is trusted? Where does spiritual authority come from? What happens when revelation appears through someone others did not expect to possess it?

The Gospel does not answer those questions by constructing another hierarchy. Instead, it repeatedly turns attention toward transformation. The measure of the path seems to be the kind of human being it produces.

## Becoming Truly Human

This may be the thread that holds the surviving Gospel of Mary together.

The Savior speaks of everything returning to its root. He describes suffering as emerging from a confusion of things that do not belong together and warns against becoming attached to what deceives us. He tells the disciples not to search anxiously “here” or “there,” because the Son of Man is within them.

Then comes a striking warning. The disciples are to proclaim the good news, but they are not to become new lawgivers. They must not take something intended to liberate and turn it into another system that constrains.

When fear overtakes them, Mary reminds them that the Savior has prepared them and made them “truly human.” Later, after their argument threatens to divide them, Levi returns to essentially the same idea: they should “put on the perfect Human.”

Spirituality in this gospel is therefore not an escape from humanity. It is a movement toward its fullness.

The path passes through the inner world. Mary speaks of the *mind*, an inner faculty capable of perceiving what ordinary sight cannot. Her vision then follows the soul as it encounters Desire, Ignorance, and a sevenfold power containing Darkness, the Kingdom of the Flesh, and forms of false and wrathful wisdom.

These powers make claims upon the soul. Desire says, “You belong to me.” Ignorance says, “You are bound.” Others accuse the soul and attempt to tell it what it is.

But the soul learns to see.

“I saw you,” it says to Desire, “but you did not see me.”

That sentence may contain the contemplative heart of the Gospel of Mary. Freedom begins with seeing. Desire can be recognized without being obeyed. Fear can be felt without becoming our identity. Anger can move through us without being mistaken for wisdom. The things that bind us may be real, but they do not necessarily reveal the deepest truth of who we are.

Again and again, the soul discovers that what appeared permanent is passing. Desire ends. Ignorance dies. Forgetfulness is revealed as temporary. The powers that seemed capable of defining the soul cannot finally possess it.

And beyond them lies rest.

Mary's vision ends in silence.

## Sitting With the Fragments

This book is not an attempt to reconstruct the Gospel of Mary. We will not fill its missing pages or pretend that we can recover everything its ancient author intended. Nor will we attempt to make Mary speak with the voice of our own century.

Instead, we will sit with what survived.

Each of the twenty-eight readings begins with a small fragment of the Gospel, usually only a few lines. We will allow those words to provide the center of gravity and then linger with them through a contemplative reflection. At the end of each reading, a short prayer will invite us to move from thinking about the text toward allowing it to become part of our own inner life.

At times Mary's language will feel strange. At other times it may feel surprisingly familiar. Desire still tells us that we belong to it. Ignorance still mistakes our bonds for our identity. Fear still narrows our vision, comparison still turns companions into rivals, and anger still knows how to disguise itself as wisdom.

But Mary's Gospel insists that these things can be seen.

And what can be seen need not always be obeyed.

Nearly half of this ancient gospel has disappeared. Perhaps that makes it an unusual text upon which to build a devotional. We cannot begin at its beginning, and in the middle of Mary's most important teaching we encounter four silent pages.

But perhaps fragments are not such a strange place to meet God.

Most of us do not receive our lives as complete manuscripts. We understand them in pieces. Some chapters make sense only much later; others seem to have gone missing entirely. We remember a few words someone spoke decades ago while forgetting whole years around them. We glimpse something true and then lose sight of it again.

The Gospel of Mary comes to us in much the same way: damaged, incomplete, and nevertheless alive.

So we will not mourn too long for the pages we do not possess. We will listen carefully to the ones that remain. We will follow Mary into questions of desire and freedom, fear and courage, inner perception and silence. We will allow ourselves not to know what cannot be known.

And perhaps, somewhere among these ancient fragments, we may discover that the voice calling the soul toward freedom has not disappeared after all.
